From the Studio

The Hands We Trust

On Craft & Making 2026
Theme Maker selection & curatorial process
Medium Lacquerwork, Vietnamese craft traditions
Year 2026

When a new brief arrives, we don't start by asking who should make it. We ask what the piece is trying to be, and whose hands already speak that language.

The difference matters. One question looks outward, toward a list of names. The other looks inward, toward the work itself. The work, it turns out, knows more than we do. If you sit with a brief long enough, it begins to point. Toward a region, a tradition, a way of working, a particular relationship between hand and material. Our task is to follow that pointing, not to override it with a decision we've already made.

A palette tells you something. A technique tells you something. The mood a space is reaching for tells you something. The weight of the commission — whether it is one piece or a hundred — tells you something else. All of this is language, and the brief is speaking it before we've said a word.

Take lacquer. In Vietnam, lacquer lives in two techniques. There is sơn ta — natural lacquer drawn from the sap of the lacquer tree, the traditional method of the North, built up in slow patient layers that need humidity to cure. And then there is what people loosely call "Japanese lacquer" — still Vietnamese craft, still the same layering by hand, but using imported industrial paint from Japan or Thailand that dries quickly in ordinary dry air. The name comes from where the paint is sourced, not from the tradition. The hands, the knowledge, the method passed down — all Vietnamese.

When a brief calls for a brighter palette, sơn ta cannot reach it. The colour has already told us where to look. When the timeline is tight — sơn ta cures slowly, week by patient week, and a production window doesn't always allow for that — the brief says it again, more clearly. It is less choosing than listening, following what the work asks for, toward the hands most likely to understand it.

Then the search begins. The same brief goes to several makers, with the same request, the same deadline, the same starting point. What comes back is never the same twice, which is the point. We are searching for sensibility, not testing performance. Whose natural aesthetic language is closest to what this brief is reaching toward? One maker moves toward fine detail. Another toward boldness of form. Another toward a restraint that suits a more minimal room. Each sample is a different reading of the same invitation, and some of those readings are simply closer to the spirit of what the work wants to be.

It feels closer to recognition than evaluation. Which hands have heard what this piece is trying to say? The sample that fits is not always the most technically impressive one. It is the one that feels, without forcing, like part of the project already, as though the maker understood something the brief never explicitly stated.

The practical realities arrive alongside all of this, and we carry them honestly. What we know of this client — how they see, what they have responded to in the past, the aesthetic instincts that show up even when they are not stated. What the operator's brand allows and what it doesn't. The season — because humidity, temperature, and rainfall shape what is genuinely possible within a given production window. The scale — because a maker who produces one extraordinary piece is not always the same maker who can sustain a hundred of them without something giving. These things matter, and we would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. But they are the landscape navigated, not the direction travelled. They follow the curatorial decision. They do not lead it.

When the real work begins

Once a maker is chosen, the brief changes character. What began as a document of intentions becomes a conversation, and it runs both ways.

A maker who has worked a material for years knows things no brief can contain. They know where sơn ta can be pushed and where it will resist. They know what happens when a colour is taken to its limit, or when a traditional technique is asked to do something it has never done before. That knowledge is among the most valuable things they bring to the work.

So the brief is shared, and then the listening begins again. We listen for the maker's instinct, for the moments when they say, softly or directly: this could go further. This could be different. What if we tried this? Those moments are where the work grows beyond what either party imagined at the start. An idea that has passed through real hands, been tested against the limits of real materials, and come out the other side more alive than it entered. Something neither the brief nor the maker could have arrived at alone.

This creative exchange is the point of the process: the risk-taking, the experimental push, the willingness to let a maker's deep knowledge redirect the original idea. Selection is the beginning of the work, not the end.

What choosing really means

By the time a choice is made, the choosing has mostly already happened. The brief pointed toward a sensibility. The samples showed whose hands already held it. The practical realities confirmed what was possible. What remains is less a decision than a recognition, a yes to what the project has been reaching toward all along. Following, not imposing.

But underneath all of this, there is something that process alone cannot hold.

When we say yes to a maker, we carry their work, and their community, into a space they may never enter. Their hands will be in rooms where guests may not know their name, may not think about the studio or workshop two hours from the city, the artist or artisan who spent months building this up, layer by careful layer. That invisibility is part of what this work is. It does not make the choice feel light.

We think of it as an introduction between craft and the world it enters, between a place and the people whose hands made it possible. When it works, a guest feels something without being able to name it. And somewhere, a maker, or a whole community of makers, continues what they have always done, now part of a conversation much larger than itself.

Every brief deserves rigour. Every choice deserves care. We try never to separate the two.
