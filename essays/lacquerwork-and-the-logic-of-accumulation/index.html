From the Studio

On Lacquerwork and the Logic of Accumulation

Fairmont Hanoi 2025 · Completed Curation, Supply & Installation
Fairmont Hanoi · Installation 2026
Client Fairmont Hotels & Resorts · Hanoi
Scope Curation · Supply · Installation
Scale 500+ original works across the property
Medium Vietnamese lacquerwork · Eggshell inlay · Traditional craft
Timeline Delivered in 8 weeks

The question was not what to put on the walls. The question was what the walls of a Fairmont in Hanoi should say to a guest who had seen hotel art in forty cities and learned to stop looking.

The answer was lacquerwork.

Partly because it is Vietnamese, but mostly because lacquer is one of the few mediums that rewards time. It reveals itself slowly. A guest who pauses at a lacquer piece on arrival will notice something on their second day that they could not have seen on the first, because the depth only emerges with the right angle of light, with a second look, with the stillness that comes once the bags have been set down and the day has been put aside.

This is the distinction between decoration and a curated artistic program. Decoration asks to be noticed once. A collection gives the guest something to return to.

The scale of the Fairmont commission — over five hundred pieces across the property — raised a curatorial question that most people frame as a logistical one: how do you maintain coherence at that volume?

Our answer was to make the medium the narrative. Vietnamese lacquerwork in its traditional form involves a process of layering — typically twenty to thirty coats applied and sanded over weeks, each coat invisible to the final eye but structurally essential to the surface that eventually emerges. The finished piece is not its top layer. It is everything underneath. We built the collection around this same logic. No single piece is the point. The accumulation is.

The works range from intimate guestroom formats, scaled for a room a guest will sleep in, to public area pieces built for the long sightlines and brief encounters of the lobby. But all of them are part of the same conversation: the relationship between surface and depth, between what is immediately visible and what takes time to arrive.

The practical challenge of five hundred pieces in eight weeks is real. It required a production network built on existing relationships with craftspeople in Hanoi workshops we had worked with before, whose quality we could vouch for and whose timelines we understood. It required a curatorial eye applied not just to selection but to quality control at volume, to the decisions that get made when a piece arrives and is not quite right and there is no time to start again.

What we learned, and have carried into every commission since, is that coherence at scale is a curatorial commitment rather than an aesthetic problem. It is made before the first piece is commissioned, held through every production decision, and visible in the finished property to anyone who knows what to look for.

The guests at the Fairmont Hanoi may not know what they are looking at. They will know that it is worth looking at. That is what a considered artistic program does.
